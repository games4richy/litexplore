Hotel Hopping in 2026: The Multi-Stay Travel Trend Taking Over and How to Do It Right

By Amara Nwosu12 min readHotels
Disclosure: Some links in this guide may earn LitExplore a small commission at no extra cost to you. That never changes what we recommend, and we only link to services we think are useful.

Picture a week in one city split between three hotels. You spend a couple of nights near the main station when you arrive, a few more in a neighborhood you'd love to wake up in, and the last nights somewhere completely different. More and more travelers now plan trips this way on purpose. Done well, you see a city from several sides instead of the same tourist loop every day.

It's called hotel hopping. In Expedia Group's Unpack '26 travel trends report, Hotels.com found that 54% of travelers book more than one hotel within a single destination on a trip. The top reasons were wanting to explore different neighborhoods (50%) and finding better deals (35%). The trend is most popular with Gen Z and millennial travelers.

It's a real change from the old habit of one hotel for the whole stay, and it can make a trip both more interesting and cheaper. Below I explain what hotel hopping is, why it has caught on, and how to plan a multi-stay trip without creating hassle for its own sake.

What Hotel Hopping Means

Hotel hopping is the practice of booking multiple different hotels during a single trip to the same destination or travel region. It is distinct from standard multi-city travel, where you visit different cities and naturally stay in different hotels because you are in different places. Hotel hopping means choosing several places to stay within one city or area on purpose.

The motivations vary by traveler type. Neighborhood explorers want to stay in different parts of a city to see it from different sides. Value seekers pay more for a convenient hotel on the first and last nights and save in between. Experience collectors want very different kinds of places, say a ryokan in Asakusa, a design hotel in Shinjuku and a small guesthouse in Shimokitazawa. Event travelers build hotel bookings around specific events, then shift their base for different days or phases of the trip.

Behind all of them is the same idea. Sticking with one hotel made sense when moving was a hassle and the hotel was simply a base to come back to each night. With phone maps, booking apps and luggage storage almost everywhere, moving is much easier now, and the payoff from seeing more of a city is bigger.

Why Hotel Hopping Has Taken Off in 2026

A few things have come together to make multi-stay trips normal, especially among younger travelers.

Booking is easy. On Hotels.com, Booking.com and Airbnb you can keep several bookings for one trip in the same account and app, with all the confirmations and check-in details in one place. Juggling three hotels is no harder than juggling one.

Luggage storage normalized. Services like Bounce and Stasher allow same-day luggage storage at hundreds of locations in most major cities, at $5 to $8 per bag for the day. You no longer have to drag your suitcase around while you wait for the next check-in. Drop your bags at 10 AM when you check out, pick them up at 3 PM, and enjoy the day in between.

Neighborhood content on social media drove demand. Instagram, TikTok, and YouTube travel content increasingly focuses on specific neighborhoods rather than generic tourist highlights. Videos about Yanaka in Tokyo, Pigneto in Rome, Laureles in Medellín or Kreuzberg in Berlin make people want to stay in those areas, not just pass through for an afternoon. Hotel hopping lets them do it.

Travelers are savvier about price. More people know that a hotel one neighborhood away from the tourist center costs much less for the same standard. Hotel hopping lets you spend more only on the nights when location really counts, like the night you arrive and the night before you fly home, and less on the rest.

The Main Types of Hotel Hopping

Type 1

Neighborhood Hopping

Staying in different parts of the same city. In Tokyo, that might be Shinjuku, Asakusa and Shimokitazawa. In London, Soho for the first nights, then Hackney or Peckham. In New York, Midtown when you arrive, then Williamsburg for the longer stretch. It works best in cities where neighborhoods feel very different and transport between them is good.

Type 2

Experience Mixing

Combining different types of accommodation for contrast and variety within a single trip. A traditional ryokan in Kyoto for two nights, then a design hotel in Osaka for three nights. A riad in Marrakech's medina, then a boutique hotel in the Palmeraie. A hostel dorm for the first sociable nights, then a private room. Where you sleep becomes part of the trip, not just a bed.

Type 3

Value Optimization

Mixing price levels on purpose. Pay for a central hotel on the nights when location matters most, such as after a long flight or before an early departure, and stay somewhere cheaper the rest of the time. For example, a $120 central hotel on nights one and seven and a $45 guesthouse for nights two to six. Compared with seven nights at the central hotel, that saves $375.

Type 4

Event-Based Hopping

Choosing hotels around what you're doing each day. Near the venue for the festival nights, near the museums for the culture days, near the park for the outdoor days. It's also the classic business-plus-leisure trip: a hotel by the conference center for the working days, then somewhere fun for the weekend.

Cities That Work Best for Hotel Hopping

Some cities suit hotel hopping much better than others. Look for neighborhoods that feel very different from each other, good public transport between them, plenty of places to stay at different prices, and a city big enough that areas really feel distinct.

Tokyo

7 to 10 nights recommended

I can't think of a better city for it. Tokyo has around 14 million people spread across dozens of very different neighborhoods, all linked by one of the world's best transit systems. Ten nights in one Tokyo hotel means seeing the city from one window. Yanaka's old shopping streets and temple graveyards feel a century away from Shimokitazawa's young, arty crowd, and neither has much in common with Ginza's luxury stores or Koenji's vintage shops and jazz bars.

The practical side is easy. A Suica card covers most trains and buses at $1 to $2 a ride, luggage delivery companies such as Yamato can send your suitcase ahead to your next hotel, usually arriving the next day, for roughly $15 to $25, and every neighborhood has hotels at every price.

2 nights: Shinjuku (arrival, transit hub) 3 nights: Asakusa (temples, traditional) 3 nights: Shimokitazawa (music, vintage) 2 nights: Ginza or Shibuya (departure)

London

7 to 10 nights

London has some of the most distinct neighborhood identities of any city in the world, and its hotel price differentials between neighborhoods are enormous. A room in Mayfair can cost three or four times as much as a similar one in Hackney, which is only half an hour or so away by train and Tube. That makes London one of the places where hotel hopping saves the most money, not just a place where it's fun.

A mixed London stay might open in Soho for the first two or three nights (expensive but central, good for Covent Garden, the West End, and initial orientation) then move to Shoreditch or Peckham for the longer second half, which is much cheaper, has great food and shows you everyday London. The Tube and Overground connect them well, and Peckham, Hackney and Dalston are some of the city's most exciting areas for food, music and independent shops.

3 nights: Soho or Covent Garden 4 nights: Shoreditch or Peckham 2 nights: wherever departs

Bangkok

6 to 8 nights

As covered in our Bangkok hotels guide, the city's BTS Skytrain makes neighborhood hopping very practical. Start in the Old City area around Banglamphu for two nights to cover the Grand Palace, Wat Pho, and the Chao Phraya river temples. Then move to On Nut or Thong Lor for the rest of the stay, for cheaper rooms, great local food and quick Skytrain access. Old riverside Bangkok and modern Skytrain Bangkok feel like different cities, and it's worth seeing both.

2 nights: Banglamphu (temples) 4 nights: On Nut or Thong Lor (BTS, local food)

Lisbon

6 to 8 nights

Lisbon has three or four distinct neighborhood experiences packed into a small, very walkable city. Alfama, the old hillside quarter of fado and narrow cobbled lanes, feels nothing like Bairro Alto's restaurants and bars. The riverside around Cais do Sodré is different again, and so is LX Factory, a former industrial complex now full of shops and studios. You might start in a traditional Alfama guesthouse (lots of stairs, no lift, amazing views) and finish in a more comfortable mid-range hotel in Príncipe Real. Both are central, but they feel completely different.

3 nights: Alfama (traditional, hilltop) 4 nights: Príncipe Real or Mouraria

The Logistics: Making Multi-Stay Travel Smooth

The obvious downside is packing up and moving. Every check-out means repacking and getting to a new place, which eats into a shorter trip. People who hotel hop often have a few habits that make it painless.

Pack light, and pack the same way every time. Experienced hoppers travel with one carry-on bag and keep everything in the same pockets. If your packing is routine, moving takes 15 minutes. If it isn't, you'll spend 45 stressful minutes stuffing things in. The less you carry, the easier every move is.

Use luggage storage services on transition days. On the day you check out of Hotel 1 and check into Hotel 2, you do not need to carry your luggage between the two check-in times. Bounce, Stasher and Vertoe partner with shops, cafes and hotels in most big cities to store bags. Book the night before, pay about $5 to $8 per bag, drop off at 10 AM and collect at 3 PM, and the day is yours.

Plan something for the gap. Check-out is usually 11 AM or noon and check-in 3 or 4 PM, so you'll have a few hours to fill however close the hotels are. Use them: a long lunch somewhere between the two, a museum, or a coffee and a wander. That way the gap becomes part of your sightseeing.

Use hotel luggage storage for early arrivals. If you get to your next hotel early, almost any hotel will keep your bags at the front desk for free. Leave them, go out, and come back when the room is ready. Staff are used to it.

When Hotel Hopping Does Not Make Sense

Sometimes one hotel for the whole stay really is better.

Short trips of three nights or less rarely benefit. Moving hotels on a trip that short uses up time you'd rather spend exploring. Save hotel hopping for trips of five nights or more.

Trips with small children or older relatives who find moving tiring. Seeing a new neighborhood is rarely worth the effort of getting everyone and everything through another check-in.

Beach and resort trips where you genuinely plan to stay at the resort most of the time. If you're there to relax and the resort is the whole point, moving just gets in the way.

Destinations without good transit. If every move needs an expensive taxi because there's no metro, the cost and hassle may not be worth it. Hotel hopping works best in cities with excellent public transport.

Starting your first hotel hop

The easiest way to try hotel hopping: on your next city trip of seven nights or more, split the stay in half. Book a central or arrival-convenient hotel for the first three nights, then book a cheaper or more characterful property in a different neighborhood for the remaining nights. Book luggage storage for moving day and plan something fun near the second hotel for the hours between check-out and check-in. If you enjoy it, try three stays next time.
