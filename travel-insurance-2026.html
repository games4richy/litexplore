Travel Insurance in 2026: Do You Need It, and Which Plan Is Worth the Money?

By Amara Nwosu 12 min read Travel Tips
Disclosure: Some links in this guide may earn LitExplore a small commission at no extra cost to you. That never changes what we recommend, and we only link to services we think are useful.

Most of the time, travel insurance feels like wasted money. You buy it, nothing happens, and you forget about it. Then one trip goes wrong. Emergency treatment abroad can cost thousands of dollars even for something routine, and a good policy turns that bill into a small excess. On that trip, the policy pays for itself many times over.

So the useful question isn't whether you need it, but what cover you need and which insurers pay out properly when you claim. The second part matters more than most people think.

What Travel Insurance Covers

A full travel insurance policy combines several kinds of cover. Look at each one separately to judge whether a policy suits your trip.

🏥

Emergency Medical Coverage

The most important part, and the one people think about least. It pays for hospital stays, doctors, emergency treatment and prescribed medicine abroad. Look for at least $100,000 of cover, and more for expensive countries such as the US, where one night in hospital can cost well over $10,000, or Switzerland. Many good policies offer $250,000 to $500,000 or more.

🚁

Emergency Medical Evacuation

Pays to fly you to a proper hospital, or home, when local care isn't good enough. An air ambulance can cost $50,000 to $200,000. This is why experienced travelers never skip insurance for adventure trips or remote places with limited hospitals. Check that it's clearly included, because some cheap plans leave it out.

✈️

Trip Cancellation and Interruption

Reimburses non-refundable trip costs if you need to cancel before departure (due to illness, family emergency, or other covered reasons) or cut your trip short. Usually it covers all of your prepaid, non-refundable costs. Some insurers sell a "Cancel for Any Reason" (CFAR) upgrade for reasons the normal policy doesn't list. It usually repays 50 to 75% of your costs, costs extra, and often has to be bought within 10 to 21 days of your first trip payment.

🧳

Baggage Loss, Damage, and Delay

Covers lost or stolen luggage (typically $1,000 to $3,000) and baggage delay (reimbursement for essential purchases when your bags are delayed more than 6 to 12 hours). Be aware that baggage limits are often low compared with what people carry. If you travel with an expensive camera, laptop or jewelry, check the limit per item before you assume it's covered.

⏱️

Travel Delay

Covers additional accommodation, meals, and transport costs when flights are delayed beyond a threshold (typically 6 to 12 hours). The daily amount is usually modest, around $100 to $200, but it adds up on long delays, and with so many flights disrupted these days it's more useful than it sounds.

When Travel Insurance Is Clearly Worth It

These are the situations where insurance clearly pays for itself.

Any international trip with significant non-refundable pre-payments. If you've booked flights ($600), accommodation ($800), and a safari or multi-day tour ($400) that are all non-refundable, that's $1,800 at risk. A good policy for a two-week trip often costs $60 to $150, depending on where you go and the level of cover. That's an easy decision.

Trips to places with very expensive or very limited healthcare. The US has the world's highest medical costs, and an uninsured visitor with a serious illness or injury can face ruinous bills. Japan, Switzerland and Australia have excellent but costly care too. At the other end, remote parts of Africa, Central Asia and rural Southeast Asia may have cheap treatment but few good hospitals, so strong evacuation cover matters most there.

Adventure travel, diving, hiking at altitude, winter sports. Many standard policies exclude "hazardous activities." If your trip involves activities in this category, you need a policy that explicitly covers them. World Nomads is a popular choice for this, and its higher Explorer plan covers a long list of activities that many insurers exclude. Check the exact activity list before buying.

Travelers with pre-existing medical conditions. Most standard policies exclude pre-existing conditions by default. If you have a health condition that might need treatment abroad, look for a policy with a pre-existing condition waiver, which is often only available if you buy within 10 to 21 days of your first trip payment, or a specialist insurer that covers declared conditions. Always declare your conditions truthfully, or a claim can be refused.

When You Might Reasonably Skip It

In some cases, the answer is less clear.

A short domestic trip with fully refundable bookings and good health coverage that extends to travel has minimal insurance value. A weekend trip where you've booked flexible-rate hotel rooms and have nothing non-refundable at stake may not justify a comprehensive policy.

Credit cards with travel insurance benefits (Chase Sapphire, Amex Platinum, many others) provide some coverage automatically when you use them to pay for travel. This is often sufficient for simple trips with limited medical risk. Card cover, though, often has lower limits, more exclusions and weaker medical benefits than a separate policy, so read the actual policy document rather than assuming it's just as good.

Even with card cover, I'd still buy a separate policy for any trip over two weeks, any trip to a destination with high healthcare costs, any trip involving adventure activities, and any trip with significant non-refundable pre-payments that your card's trip cancellation benefit may not fully cover.

Providers Worth Knowing in 2026

One important point first: who can sell you a policy depends on the country you live in, not on your passport. Several of the providers below, including Travelex, Travel Guard and Trawick, mainly sell to US residents. If you live in Nigeria, compare travel policies from licensed Nigerian insurers and brokers, and for Schengen and UK visas, check that the policy meets the embassy's requirements.

Best Overall

Travelex Insurance

Best for most travelers

A well-known US provider that regularly ranks near the top in independent comparisons. Its plans have a simple structure and a straightforward claims process. The Travel Basic plan is a low-cost starting point, and Travel Select is the fuller plan most travelers will want. Check the medical limits carefully and add upgrades if you need more.

Adventure Travel

World Nomads

Best for active travelers and long-term trips

Popular with people planning activities other insurers won't cover, such as diving, surfing, trekking and winter sports. There are two plans, Standard and Explorer, with Explorer covering more activities. It sells to residents of many countries, and you can often buy or extend cover while you're already traveling. It can cost more for a basic trip, but it's worth it for adventure travel.

Pre-Existing Conditions

Travel Guard (AIG)

Best for travelers with medical history

AIG's travel brand, often suggested for travelers with pre-existing conditions who buy within the waiver window. It also suits complicated multi-country trips, and its plans range from trip cancellation to emergency evacuation.

Budget Option

Trawick International

Best for budget travelers needing flexibility

Offers plans such as Safe Travels Explorer at lower prices than many big brands, with reasonable medical and cancellation cover. A good fit for cheaper trips where evacuation cover is the main concern.

Frequent Travelers

Allianz Travel (Annual Plan)

Best for 3+ trips per year

If you take three or more international trips per year, an annual multi-trip policy typically costs less than buying separate single-trip policies. Allianz Travel's annual plans cover all trips within a 12-month period up to a specified per-trip duration (usually 30 to 45 days per trip). You buy once and you're covered all year.

The Timing Rule That Most People Miss

Buy your policy within a day or two of booking your flights, not the week before you leave. If you're already insured when something happens, say you fall ill or a relative does, you can claim for cancelling. If you only buy three weeks before departure and it happened before that, it isn't covered. The weeks right after booking are when cover is most valuable.

What Travel Insurance Typically Does NOT Cover

Knowing what isn't covered matters as much as knowing what is. Read the exclusions before you buy, not when you're trying to claim.

Pre-existing medical conditions (unless you purchased a waiver within the required window). This is one of the most common reasons claims are turned down. Most policies define "pre-existing" broadly, often as any condition you've been diagnosed with, treated for or sought advice about in a set period before buying. Read the definition in your policy.

Injuries while intoxicated. Most policies won't pay if you were hurt while drunk or under the influence of drugs, and insurers apply this rule strictly.

Known events at time of purchase. If you buy a policy after a hurricane has been named and it then hits your destination, it isn't covered, because it was already a "known event." Another reason to buy early.

Travel advisories. If your home government has issued a "do not travel" advisory for your destination before your trip, some policies void coverage or refuse to pay claims related to the situation that generated the advisory.

Extreme sports or hazardous activities unless explicitly covered. Check the exact list in your policy. "Hiking" is usually covered. "Mountaineering above 4,500 meters" often isn't, without a specific add-on.

How to Make a Claim

Most claim problems come down to paperwork, not cover. Insurers need specific documents, and if you don't collect them at the time, your claim may be refused or cut. Here's what to gather while it's happening.

For medical claims: get a written medical report from the treating facility, an itemized bill, proof of payment receipts, and a statement from the doctor if you were advised not to travel. Get these before you leave the hospital, because they're much harder to get later.

For baggage claims: file a Property Irregularity Report (PIR) with the airline at the airport before leaving, get a reference number, photograph the damaged or missing items and their contents, and keep all receipts for emergency replacement purchases.

For trip cancellation: get written documentation of the cancellation reason (doctor's certificate if medical, death certificate if bereavement, employer letter if job loss), and documentation of all non-refundable costs.

Tell your insurer as soon as you can, since many policies set short deadlines for certain claims. In a medical emergency, call the 24-hour emergency assistance line, ideally before you agree to expensive treatment.

My Simple Answer to "Do You Need It?"

Yes, for any trip abroad longer than a few days or with non-refundable bookings. A policy typically costs around 4 to 8% of your trip. A medical emergency, a forced cancellation or an evacuation can cost many times more than the trip itself. Buy within a couple of days of booking flights, check that your destination and activities are covered, and save the emergency assistance number in your phone.
